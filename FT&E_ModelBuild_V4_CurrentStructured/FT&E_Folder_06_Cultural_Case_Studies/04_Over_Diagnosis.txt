Over-diagnosis refers to the identification of medical conditions that, if left undetected, would not have caused symptoms or harm during a person's lifetime. This phenomenon can lead to unnecessary interventions, additional costs, and potential harm to patients. Although the causes and consequences of over-diagnosis vary across different healthcare systems, the underlying mechanisms can be understood through the lens of Forgiveness, Time, and Emergence (FT&E). By exploring how FT&E applies to the United States and the United Kingdom, we can see how unresolved contradictions within each system give rise to cycles of over-diagnosis and overtreatment.

Forgiveness, Time, and Emergence (FT&E) is a framework suggesting that stable outcomes in any system rely on three interconnected processes. Forgiveness is the ability of a system to absorb, correct, or integrate errors or contradictions. Time is the iterative process that allows gradual resolution of those contradictions. Emergence is the new stable state that arises once contradictions are successfully integrated or neutralized. When any element of FT&E is lacking, contradictions tend to accumulate, eventually destabilizing the system. Over-diagnosis exemplifies what happens when medical and societal contradictions go unresolved, driving the system to pathologize normal variations in health or detect low-risk conditions as serious problems.

1. Over-diagnosis in the United States

In the United States, a fee-for-service model often incentivizes healthcare providers to offer more tests and treatments, which can lead to over-diagnosis. This model is not driven purely by conscious greed; it is a structural contradiction: a profit-oriented system collides with the ethical goal of providing necessary care. The result is a cycle in which more diagnoses lead to more treatments, which in turn reinforce the expectation that every possible condition must be identified and managed.

a) Example: Prostate Cancer Screening  
PSA (prostate-specific antigen) testing for prostate cancer frequently identifies slow-growing tumors that would not cause symptoms or mortality. Many men receive aggressive treatment (such as surgery or radiation) with side effects like incontinence or sexual dysfunction, without a clear survival benefit. The system lacks a mechanism of forgiveness for benign or slow-growing tumors. Instead of waiting (Time) to see if the tumor poses real harm, immediate intervention occurs, often causing new contradictions like side effects and increased healthcare costs.

b) Defensive Medicine and Litigation Concerns  
US physicians may over-diagnose to avoid malpractice lawsuits. Without forgiveness for clinical uncertainty, doctors rush to label every symptom or abnormal test result as a condition. Over time, this leads to more diagnoses, more interventions, and a societal expectation that all health deviations must be treated. The contradiction between clinical uncertainty and legal pressure remains unresolved, fueling the cycle of over-diagnosis.

c) FT&E Implications  
Forgiveness is absent because physicians and patients have little tolerance for uncertainty or benign neglect of minor abnormalities. Time is compressed by financial pressures and patient demands for quick answers. Emergence, in this case, becomes an endless cascade of interventions and new contradictions rather than a stable resolution.

2. Over-diagnosis in the United Kingdom (NHS)

In the UK, the National Health Service (NHS) operates on a universal healthcare model. While this removes the profit motive that exists in the US, over-diagnosis still occurs due to other contradictions, such as public expectations, resource limitations, and defensive practices.

a) Example: Mental Health Diagnoses  
In recent years, there has been a marked increase in diagnoses for conditions such as anxiety, depression, ADHD, and autism. While awareness of mental health is beneficial, the system sometimes lacks the ability to distinguish between natural human variation and true pathology. Public demand for quick identification and support, combined with finite NHS resources, can lead to diagnostic inflation. The contradiction between universal coverage and limited specialist availability remains unresolved, pushing doctors to provide diagnoses to justify access to services.

b) Screening Programs and Early Intervention  
The NHS encourages preventive care, including screenings for various conditions. While this can save lives, it also leads to detecting slow-growing or benign conditions that would not have caused harm. The contradiction is that universal screening may appear beneficial, but it can produce unnecessary labeling and overtreatment. Because the system does not always incorporate forgiveness (acknowledging that some detected conditions might never progress), time alone increases the likelihood of unnecessary procedures. Emergence, in this scenario, becomes more interventions rather than the stable integration of findings.

c) FT&E Implications  
In the NHS, forgiveness is restricted by the expectation of consistent, comprehensive care for all. Time, as a mechanism for watchful waiting, often clashes with public and political pressure to act swiftly. Emergence then materializes as greater diagnostic reach, which can solve some problems while creating new contradictions, such as overburdened clinics and overtreated patients.

3. The Deeper FT&E Explanation of Over-diagnosis

Forgiveness: In healthcare, forgiveness would manifest as tolerance for normal health variations, acknowledgment that not every detected abnormality requires immediate intervention, and acceptance of uncertainty. When forgiveness is absent, every slight deviation is medicalized.

Time: This refers to allowing gradual resolution or observation. Without enough time, doctors rush to label and treat. Time also allows the body or mind to self-correct, but compressed time frames and system pressures short-circuit this natural process.

Emergence: The system either transitions to a stable state where true pathologies are distinguished from benign variations, or it becomes locked in a loop of identifying more and more conditions, fueling new contradictions.

5. Concluding Thoughts

Over-diagnosis is not merely a medical or economic problem; it is a manifestation of unresolved contradictions in healthcare systems. In the US, it arises from a profit-driven model and defensive practices. In the UK, it emerges from universal coverage expectations, resource limitations, and public demand for swift identification. Across both, the absence of true forgiveness (acceptance of uncertainty and benign variations), the compression of time (rushing to intervene), and the lack of stable emergence (piling on diagnoses without resolving underlying issues) ensure that over-diagnosis persists.

By recognizing how FT&E applies to healthcare, stakeholders can design policies that incorporate forgiveness (acknowledging that not all abnormalities require immediate treatment), allow adequate time (watchful waiting where appropriate), and promote healthy emergence (long-term stability rather than short-term reactive interventions). This would reduce unnecessary interventions, lower costs, and ultimately improve patient well-being.




### How Doctors Currently Handle This:
1. **Biopsies and Testing:** When a suspicious tumor is found, doctors often perform biopsies to analyze its naturewhether its benign, slow-growing, or aggressive.
2. **Grading and Staging:** Tumors are graded based on how abnormal the cells look and staged based on how much they have spread.
3. **Active Surveillance vs. Immediate Treatment:** For some cancers (like certain prostate and thyroid cancers), "watchful waiting" or "active surveillance" is recommended. This means doctors monitor the tumor rather than immediately treating it.
4. **Treatment Decision Based on Risk:** If tests indicate the cancer is aggressive, immediate treatment is pursued. If its slow-growing, treatment may be deferred to avoid unnecessary side effects.

### Why Over-Diagnosis Still Happens:
- **Screening Detects Very Early Cancers:** Modern imaging and screening programs can find tiny tumors that might never grow or cause harm. However, because of fear and uncertainty, many patients (and doctors) opt to treat them anyway.
- **Lack of a Perfect Predictive Model:** While doctors use biomarkers, imaging, and pathology to estimate risk, there's no perfect way to predict exactly which cancers will become dangerous.
- **Medical and Legal Pressures:** Some doctors may recommend treatment out of caution or to avoid potential malpractice claims, even if the risk is low.

### Where FT&E Fits In:
- **Forgiveness (Tolerance for Uncertainty):** Instead of immediately assuming every tumor is deadly, medicine needs a structured way to identify which ones truly need intervention. This is already happening in some fields but is not yet universal.
- **Time (Monitoring Before Acting):** Active surveillance programs represent an attempt to integrate time into medical decision-making. The challenge is making sure they are applied where appropriate and not dismissed due to patient anxiety or legal risks.
- **Emergence (Better Predictive Models):** AI and advanced diagnostics may help refine our understanding of which cancers will become aggressive and which will not. The ideal system would balance early detection with targeted intervention, avoiding unnecessary harm.

### Is the Advice Still Sound?
Yes, but with nuance. The argument isnt that we should avoid treating cancer, but rather that:
1. **Not all detected tumors need immediate treatment.** Over-treatment of non-threatening cancers causes harm.
2. **More research is needed to refine predictive models.** AI and genetic profiling could improve our ability to tell which tumors need urgent intervention.
3. **The system should incorporate FT&E principles.** Forgiveness (accepting uncertainty), time (monitoring), and emergence (better diagnostic tools) could reduce unnecessary treatments while still catching aggressive cancers in time.
