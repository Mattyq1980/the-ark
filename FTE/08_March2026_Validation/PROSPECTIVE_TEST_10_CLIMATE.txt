STEP 2: OPERATOR DEFINITIONS (before seeing data)
================================================================================

𝔉 (FORGIVENESS) — Climate system buffering capacity

  In climate science, 𝔉 is the system's capacity to absorb
  radiative forcing perturbations and return toward equilibrium
  without triggering irreversible state changes.

  - Ocean thermal inertia: The ocean absorbs ~90% of excess heat,
    acting as a massive thermal buffer. Deep ocean circulation
    distributes heat globally. High thermal inertia = high 𝔉.
  - Carbon sinks: Oceans and terrestrial biosphere absorb ~50%
    of anthropogenic CO2 emissions. Active carbon sinks = high 𝔉.
  - Negative feedbacks: Planck radiation (hotter surface radiates
    more energy), silicate weathering (warmer climate accelerates
    rock weathering which draws down CO2 over geological time),
    cloud formation (some cloud types increase albedo). These
    resist perturbations = 𝔉 mechanisms.
  - Ice/snow albedo: Reflective surfaces cool the planet. But
    this is also a POSITIVE feedback — warming melts ice, reducing
    albedo, causing more warming. Ice-albedo acts as 𝔉 when
    stable but becomes an anti-𝔉 amplifier when destabilised.

  Higher 𝔉 = greater capacity to absorb forcing without
  catastrophic state change.

  𝔉 = 0 prediction: If the climate system had ZERO buffering
  capacity — no ocean thermal inertia, no carbon sinks, no
  negative feedbacks — then any perturbation in radiative forcing
  would immediately and fully manifest as temperature change.
  The system would be maximally sensitive, with no lag between
  forcing and response. Climate sensitivity would be extreme.
  Small volcanic eruptions or solar variations would produce
  enormous temperature swings. The climate would be wildly
  unstable.

  𝔉 = ∞ prediction: If the climate system had INFINITE buffering
  — all perturbations absorbed with no response — then no amount
  of greenhouse gas emissions would produce warming, no volcanic
  winter would produce cooling, no orbital variation would
  trigger ice ages. The system would be completely inert.
  But this would also mean no weather, no seasons as dynamic
  responses — a dead system with no emergent climate patterns.
  Infinite buffering prevents any emergent response.

  Proxy variable: Climate sensitivity (°C per doubling of CO2),
  ocean heat uptake efficiency, carbon sink capacity, feedback
  parameter (W/m² per °C).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

T (TIME) — Climate response timescale

  The climate system operates across dramatically different
  timescales, and the relationship between forcing timescale
  and response timescale determines outcomes:

  T_too_fast: Forcing that changes faster than the system can
  respond. Volcanic eruptions inject aerosols in days — faster
  than ocean circulation can redistribute heat, producing
  temporary cooling spikes. Abrupt methane release (clathrate
  gun hypothesis) would force the system faster than negative
  feedbacks can compensate. When forcing outpaces response,
  the system exhibits transient extremes before equilibrating.

  T_viable: Forcing that changes on timescales the system can
  track. Orbital (Milankovitch) cycles operate over 20,000–
  100,000 years — slow enough for ice sheets, ocean circulation,
  and carbon cycle to respond coherently. The system produces
  orderly glacial-interglacial cycles. This is the T-window for
  stable climate state transitions.

  T_too_slow: When the system responds too slowly relative to
  the forcing, disequilibrium accumulates. Current anthropogenic
  CO2 emissions are forcing the system at a rate that exceeds
  the ocean's capacity to absorb and redistribute heat on
  decadal timescales. The "committed warming" phenomenon —
  warming that will occur even if emissions stop today — is
  a measure of accumulated T_too_slow disequilibrium.

  Proxy variable: Climate response time, equilibrium climate
  sensitivity vs. transient climate response, ocean mixing
  timescale, ice sheet response time.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ΔC (CONTRADICTION LOAD) — Radiative forcing perturbation

  The "contradiction" in climate terms is any perturbation to
  the Earth's energy balance that forces the system away from
  its current state — requiring adjustment.

  - Greenhouse gas increase: Additional CO2, CH4, N2O alter the
    radiative balance, trapping more outgoing longwave radiation.
    Each doubling of CO2 = ~3.7 W/m² of radiative forcing.
  - Volcanic eruptions: Inject sulphate aerosols into the
    stratosphere, reflecting incoming solar radiation, producing
    temporary cooling. Large eruptions (Tambora 1815, Pinatubo
    1991) = large acute ΔC.
  - Orbital variations: Milankovitch cycles change the
    distribution and intensity of solar radiation reaching
    Earth. Slow, periodic ΔC that drives glacial cycles.
  - Solar variability: Changes in solar output (11-year cycle,
    longer trends) alter total energy input.

  SUPPRESSED ΔC: Forcing that accumulates without the system
  fully responding — building toward a threshold where abrupt
  state change occurs. Climate tipping points are the canonical
  example: gradual warming approaches a threshold (e.g., ice
  sheet stability limit), beyond which positive feedbacks
  dominate and the system shifts rapidly and potentially
  irreversibly to a new state. The forcing was gradual but the
  response is sudden because the ΔC was suppressed by the
  system's buffering until the buffer was overwhelmed.

  Proxy variable: Radiative forcing (W/m²), CO2 concentration
  (ppm), global mean temperature anomaly, ocean heat content.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

E* (EMERGENCE) — Climate patterns and states

  The climate system produces emergent phenomena that cannot be
  derived from any single component:

  - ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation): A coupled ocean-
    atmosphere oscillation in the tropical Pacific that
    affects weather globally. Neither ocean nor atmosphere
    alone produces ENSO — it emerges from their interaction.
  - Monsoon systems: Large-scale seasonal circulation patterns
    driven by land-sea thermal contrast, moisture transport,
    and topography. Emergent at the continental scale.
  - Glacial-interglacial cycles: The regular alternation of
    ice ages and warm periods driven by orbital forcing
    amplified by carbon cycle and ice-albedo feedbacks. The
    ~100,000-year cycle is emergent — Milankovitch forcing
    alone is too weak to explain the magnitude of response.
  - Jet streams and Hadley cells: Large-scale atmospheric
    circulation patterns that emerge from differential heating,
    Earth's rotation, and fluid dynamics.
  - Climate states: The "icehouse" and "greenhouse" states of
    Earth history represent qualitatively different climate
    regimes — emergent system-level configurations.

  Key prediction: Climate emergence is genuinely novel — ENSO,
  monsoons, and glacial cycles are system-level phenomena that
  cannot be derived from component properties alone.

  Proxy variable: ENSO index, monsoon indices, glacial cycle
  periodicity, atmospheric circulation patterns.

  a) Forcing that is too fast for the system to equilibrate

     dips that the system eventually recovers from but which cause

  b) Forcing on viable timescales (Milankovitch orbital cycles)

     tracks the forcing coherently because T is within the
     viable window.

  c) When the system responds too slowly relative to forcing,
     "committed warming" accumulates — warming that is locked in
     but not yet realised because ocean thermal inertia has
     absorbed the energy. This thermal debt should be documented
     in climate science as a T_too_slow phenomenon. The longer
     the delay between forcing and full response, the larger the
     accumulated disequilibrium.

  d) There should be evidence of a non-monotonic optimum: climate
     response is not simply "faster is better" or "slower is
     better" — the system needs to respond on appropriate
     timescales for each type of forcing.
