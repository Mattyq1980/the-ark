𝔉 (FORGIVENESS) — Thermal energy / molecular mobility

  In phase transition physics, 𝔉 is the system's capacity to
  ACCOMMODATE molecular rearrangement — to allow molecules to
  explore configurations, overcome energy barriers, and find new
  structural arrangements.

  - Thermal energy (kT): Higher temperature means molecules have
    more kinetic energy to overcome intermolecular bonds and move
    into new configurations. This is the primary 𝔉 mechanism.
  - Entropy: The drive toward disorder that resists rigid ordering.
    At high T, entropy dominates and the system remains fluid.
  - Fluctuations: Thermal fluctuations allow the system to sample
    different configurations, enabling transitions when conditions
    favour them.

  Higher 𝔉 = higher molecular mobility = greater capacity to
  rearrange without fracturing or getting stuck.

  𝔉 = 0 prediction: At absolute zero (0 K), thermal energy
  vanishes. Molecules cannot rearrange. No further phase transitions
  can occur through thermal means. The system is frozen in whatever
  state it reached — a "glass" if disordered, a perfect crystal if
  ordered. The third law of thermodynamics should confirm this.

  𝔉 = ∞ prediction: At extreme temperature, all bonds are broken,
  all structure is destroyed. Matter becomes plasma — no molecular
  structure, no crystals, no liquid order. Pure kinetic chaos with
  no coherent emergent structure.

  Proxy variable: Temperature, thermal energy (kT), molecular
  diffusion rate, relaxation time.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

T (TIME) — Processing duration / equilibration timescale

  Phase transitions occur over characteristic timescales that
  determine the outcome:

  T_too_fast: Extremely rapid cooling (quenching) can prevent
  equilibrium phase transitions from occurring. The system doesn't
  have time to nucleate and grow the equilibrium phase — it gets
  trapped in a metastable, disordered state (glass formation).
  Too fast = structure frozen before processing completes.

  T_viable: Moderate cooling/heating rates allow nucleation and
  crystal growth to proceed. The system has sufficient time to
  explore configurations and find the equilibrium structure. This
  produces well-ordered crystals with the expected thermodynamic
  properties.

  T_too_slow: Extremely slow processes near critical points can
  lead to "critical slowing down" — the system takes increasingly
  long to equilibrate as correlation lengths diverge. But in most
  cases, very slow cooling produces the most perfect equilibrium
  structures (large single crystals, well-defined phases).

  Proxy variable: Cooling/heating rate, nucleation time,
  equilibration time, annealing duration.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ΔC (CONTRADICTION LOAD) — Driving force for phase change

  The "contradiction" in a phase transition is the thermodynamic
  driving force — the difference between the system's current state
  and its equilibrium state.

  - Undercooling (ΔT below melting point): The liquid "should" be
    solid but hasn't transformed yet. The further below the melting
    point, the greater the thermodynamic driving force.
  - Superheating (ΔT above boiling point): The liquid "should" be
    gas but hasn't boiled yet.
  - Supersaturation: A solution contains more dissolved solute than
    the equilibrium concentration. The excess "should" precipitate
    but hasn't yet.
  - Free energy difference (ΔG): The quantitative measure of how
    far the system is from equilibrium — the magnitude of the
    "contradiction" between current state and equilibrium state.

  SUPPRESSED ΔC: Metastable states where the driving force BUILDS
  without release. Supercooled water at −20°C has enormous
  thermodynamic driving force toward ice, but the energy barrier
  to nucleation prevents it. The contradiction accumulates silently
  until a perturbation triggers nucleation — then crystallisation
  proceeds explosively.

  Proxy variable: Degree of undercooling/superheating,
  supersaturation ratio, free energy difference (ΔG).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

E* (EMERGENCE) — New phase / new macroscopic order

  Phase transitions produce qualitatively new macroscopic states:

  - Crystalline order: Liquid → solid produces a regular lattice
    structure that did not exist in the disordered liquid.
  - Magnetic ordering: Paramagnetic → ferromagnetic produces
    spontaneous magnetisation — a macroscopic property that does
    not exist in the disordered phase.
  - Superconductivity: Below the critical temperature, electrical
    resistance drops to exactly zero. A qualitatively new property.
  - Bose-Einstein condensation: Multiple particles occupy a single
    quantum state — a macroscopic quantum phenomenon.
  - Superfluidity: Liquid flows without any viscosity — a property
    that is qualitatively absent above the transition temperature.

  Key prediction: The new phase has macroscopic properties that
  CANNOT be found in the old phase. Ferromagnetism does not exist
  in the paramagnetic state. Superconductivity does not exist above
  Tc. Crystalline order does not exist in the liquid. E* is
  genuinely emergent — not a gradual quantitative change but a
  qualitative discontinuity in the system's properties.

  Proxy variable: Order parameter (magnetisation, crystal symmetry,
  superfluid density, Cooper pair density), macroscopic property
  change.
