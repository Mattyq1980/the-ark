STEP 2: OPERATOR DEFINITIONS (before seeing data)
================================================================================

𝔉 (FORGIVENESS) — Neural flexibility / synaptic modifiability

  In neuroscience, 𝔉 is the brain's capacity to MODIFY its own
  connections — to strengthen, weaken, form, or eliminate synapses
  in response to experience. This is the neural system's ability
  to accommodate new information without catastrophic disruption.

  - Synaptic plasticity: The ability of synapses to change
    strength. Higher plasticity = more capacity to reorganise
    circuits in response to new input.
  - Neurotransmitter availability: Sufficient neurotransmitter
    supply and receptor sensitivity allow signals to be processed
    and integrated. Depletion reduces the system's capacity to
    respond.
  - Neurogenesis / dendritic branching: The brain's ability to
    grow new connections and even new neurons in some regions
    (hippocampus) — structural 𝔉 at the anatomical level.
  - Neuromodulation: Systems like dopamine, serotonin, and
    norepinephrine that REGULATE plasticity — they set the
    gain on how much the system can change in response to input.

  Higher 𝔉 = greater capacity to rewire, adapt, learn, integrate
  new experience into existing neural architecture.

  𝔉 = 0 prediction: If synaptic plasticity is completely
  eliminated, no new learning can occur. The brain is locked into
  its current wiring. Existing memories persist but no new ones
  form. No adaptation to new stimuli. The neural system is
  frozen — analogous to the thermodynamic 𝔉 = 0 in Test #7.
  This should correspond to known neurological conditions or
  pharmacological states where plasticity is abolished.

  𝔉 = ∞ prediction: If plasticity is unlimited and unregulated,
  every input overwrites previous connections. No stable memories
  form because everything is constantly being rewritten. The
  system cannot maintain coherent structure. Pure noise — no
  learning, because learning requires SELECTIVE strengthening
  and weakening, not wholesale rewiring. Too much 𝔉 = chaos,
  no stable emergence.

  Proxy variable: Synaptic plasticity measures (LTP magnitude,
  BDNF levels, receptor density), neuromodulator levels,
  neurogenesis rate, critical period status.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

T (TIME) — Processing duration / consolidation timescale

  Neural learning and memory occur over characteristic timescales
  that determine the outcome:

  T_too_fast: A single brief exposure may not produce lasting
  change. Flash exposure to a stimulus — too fast to consolidate.
  Working memory (seconds) does not automatically become long-term
  memory. The system needs time to process.

  T_viable: Repeated exposure, spaced practice, sleep
  consolidation. The brain requires TIME to transfer information
  from short-term hippocampal storage to long-term cortical
  storage. This process involves replay, reconsolidation, and
  structural synaptic change. Spaced repetition should be more
  effective than massed practice — the T-window for optimal
  learning.

  T_too_slow: In the context of critical periods — windows of
  heightened plasticity that close with development — if the
  appropriate input doesn't arrive during the critical period,
  the circuit may never develop properly (e.g., visual cortex
  development requires visual input during early life). The
  window closes. T has expired.

  Proxy variable: Consolidation time, sleep cycles, spacing
  intervals, critical period duration, exposure duration.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ΔC (CONTRADICTION LOAD) — Neural signal requiring processing

  The "contradiction" in neural terms is any input that doesn't
  match the brain's current model — a prediction error, a novel
  stimulus, a traumatic experience, a conflict between competing
  neural representations.

  - Prediction error: The brain operates on predictive coding —
    it generates expectations and compares them to actual input.
    The DIFFERENCE between prediction and reality is the
    prediction error signal. This is the neural ΔC — the
    contradiction between what the system expects and what it
    receives.
  - Novel stimuli: New, unexpected, or salient inputs that
    require the system to update its model.
  - Traumatic input: Extreme experiences that overwhelm the
    system's normal processing capacity — the neural equivalent
    of ΔC exceeding 𝔉 bandwidth.
  - Conflicting memories / cognitive dissonance: When two
    neural representations are mutually incompatible, the
    system must resolve or suppress the conflict.

  SUPPRESSED ΔC: Traumatic memory that is not processed —
  encoded but not integrated into the normal memory system.
  PTSD should be the canonical example: traumatic experience
  stored in a hyperactive, unintegrated form that repeatedly
  re-activates (flashbacks, hypervigilance) because the ΔC was
  never metabolised through normal consolidation. The
  contradiction sits in the system, unprocessed, causing
  ongoing disruption.

  Proxy variable: Prediction error magnitude, stimulus novelty,
  trauma severity, cognitive dissonance, amygdala activation.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

E* (EMERGENCE) — Learned behaviour / stable memory / adapted
                  neural architecture

  Neural processing produces qualitatively new capabilities:

  - Long-term memory: A stable representation that did not exist
    before the experience. New knowledge, new skills, new
    associations.
  - Learned behaviour: Motor skills, language, habits — entire
    behavioural repertoires that emerge from synaptic modification
    and are not present in the naive brain.
  - Adapted circuitry: Structural changes in dendritic branching,
    spine density, myelination — the physical rewiring of the
    brain that constitutes learning at the anatomical level.
  - Personality / expertise: The accumulated product of a lifetime
    of neural E* — the person you become through experience.

  Key prediction: Neural E* is genuinely emergent — it cannot be
  found in the pre-learning state of the brain. A memory does not
  exist "weakly" before it is formed. A skill is not present in
  reduced form before training. The learning event creates
  something new in the neural substrate.

  Proxy variable: Memory test performance, skill acquisition,
  structural brain changes (imaging), behavioural adaptation.

P1: 𝔉 = 0 → NO NEW LEARNING; 𝔉 = ∞ → NO STABLE MEMORY (I1, I5)

  When synaptic plasticity is eliminated (𝔉 = 0), FT&E predicts:

  a) No new memories can form. Existing memories and skills
     persist (they are already structurally encoded) but the
     system cannot create new ones. This should correspond to
     documented conditions — pharmacological blockade of
     plasticity mechanisms, or specific brain lesions — that
     produce exactly this pattern: intact old memories,
     inability to form new ones.

  b) Conversely, excessive unregulated plasticity (𝔉 → ∞)
     should prevent stable memory formation. If every new input
     overwrites previous synaptic changes, nothing consolidates.
     This should manifest in conditions where plasticity
     mechanisms are pathologically overactive — potentially
     excitotoxicity, seizure states, or pharmacological
     overstimulation.

  c) Between these extremes, there should be an OPTIMAL
     plasticity range where learning is maximised — enough
     flexibility to encode new information, enough stability
     to retain it. This is the neural 𝔉 bandwidth.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

P2: OPTIMAL T WINDOW — SPACING AND SLEEP MATTER (I3, I9)

  FT&E predicts a non-monotonic relationship between processing
  time and learning quality:

  a) Too fast: Massed practice (cramming) should be less effective
     than spaced practice for long-term retention. A single
     intense study session should produce weaker long-term memory
     than the same total time distributed over multiple sessions
     with gaps. The brain needs temporal gaps to consolidate.

  b) Optimal rate: Sleep should be CRITICAL for memory
     consolidation. During sleep, the brain should replay and
     consolidate experiences from the day. Sleep deprivation
     should impair new memory formation. The T-window for
     optimal learning includes obligatory rest/consolidation
     phases.

  c) Critical periods: There should be developmental windows
     during which specific types of learning are dramatically
     enhanced — and after which they become much harder or
     impossible. Visual development, language acquisition, and
     other fundamental capacities should have critical periods
     that CLOSE. Missing the window = T expired.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

P3: SUPPRESSED ΔC → PATHOLOGICAL REACTIVATION (I4, I7)

  FT&E predicts that traumatic experience that is NOT properly
  processed will produce pathological, recurring disruption:

  a) PTSD: Traumatic memories that are not integrated through
     normal consolidation should remain in a hyperactive,
     unprocessed state. They should INVOLUNTARILY reactivate —
     flashbacks, nightmares, hypervigilance — because the
     contradiction (ΔC) was never metabolised. The system keeps
     trying to process what it hasn't been able to process.

  b) The severity of PTSD symptoms should correlate with the
     magnitude of the unprocessed ΔC — more severe trauma =
     more intense and persistent symptoms.

  c) Effective PTSD treatments should work by enabling the
     brain to FINALLY PROCESS the suppressed ΔC — either by
     reopening the memory for reconsolidation (exposure therapy,
     EMDR) or by enhancing the 𝔉 bandwidth so the system can
     handle the contradiction (pharmacological augmentation
     of plasticity during therapy).

  d) There should be a neural equivalent of the "explosive
     release" pattern: kindling in epilepsy — where repeated
     sub-threshold stimulation progressively LOWERS the seizure
     threshold until full seizures occur spontaneously. This is
     accumulated neural ΔC that eventually overwhelms the
     system's suppression capacity.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

P4: LEARNING PRODUCES GENUINELY NOVEL NEURAL STRUCTURE (I6, I10, I11)

  FT&E predicts that learning creates qualitatively new neural
  architecture:

  a) Long-term memory formation should involve STRUCTURAL changes
     in synapses — not just temporary electrical changes but
     physical modification of dendritic spines, receptor
     populations, and even new synaptic connections. The
     neural architecture after learning is physically different
     from before.

  b) Expertise should produce measurable brain differences.
     Musicians, taxi drivers, athletes, mathematicians —
     people who have undergone extensive training in specific
     domains should show structural brain differences in
     relevant regions compared to non-experts. The brain
     literally reshapes itself through learning.

  c) The learned state is NOT a return to a prior condition.
     Memory is not "remembering" something the brain already
     knew. Skill is not "recovering" a capacity the brain
     already had. Learning creates something new — a
     representation, a circuit, a capability that did not
     previously exist. E* ≠ prior state.

  d) The product of learning should be a property of the
     NETWORK, not of individual neurons. No single neuron
     "knows" a memory or "has" a skill. Knowledge and skill
     emerge from patterns of connectivity across populations
     of neurons. This is neural emergence.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

P5: 𝔉 BANDWIDTH — NEUROMODULATION SETS THE RANGE (I2, I8)

  FT&E predicts that the brain has regulatory mechanisms that
  control the bandwidth of neural plasticity:

  a) Neuromodulatory systems (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine,
     acetylcholine) should GATE plasticity — determining when
     and where the brain is most modifiable. High dopamine in
     response to reward should enhance learning of rewarded
     behaviours. High norepinephrine in response to stress
     should enhance encoding of threatening stimuli.

  b) There should be mechanisms that PROTECT existing memories
     from being overwritten by new learning — a stability-
     plasticity balance. The brain cannot be maximally plastic
     everywhere simultaneously or old memories would be
     destroyed by new ones. This is the stability-plasticity
     dilemma, and the brain should have specific mechanisms
     (synaptic tagging, systems consolidation) to manage it.

  c) Critical periods should be controlled by specific
     molecular mechanisms that OPEN and CLOSE plasticity
     windows. The end of a critical period = closure of
     the 𝔉 bandwidth for that circuit. It should be possible
     to experimentally REOPEN critical periods by manipulating
     these mechanisms — demonstrating that the bandwidth
     limit is regulatory, not absolute.

  d) Pathological narrowing of 𝔉 bandwidth should produce
     rigidity — inability to adapt, perseveration, cognitive
     inflexibility. This should be observed in
     neurodegeneration and aging, where plasticity mechanisms
     decline.
